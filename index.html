    This is only an estimate.How it works

    It uses typical calories for each item’s type, quantity, and unit, and leans low on purpose: lower-end calorie counts and smaller package sizes. Actual calories vary by brand and size, so use it as a rough starting point for planning, not an exact count.

    Besides each type’s own units, it understands weights (lb, oz, kg, and g), kitchen measures (cups, quarts, and liters), servings, and sizes written into the unit, like “25 lb bags” or “24-pack”. “Each”, or no unit, counts as one of the smallest container the item comes in.

    Items added by scanning a barcode use the calories from their own label instead, when Open Food Facts lists them. Those show “from the label” rather than “est.”.

    Not counted: expired items, infant formula and baby food, and things with few or no calories, like fuel, cookware, seeds, spices, coffee, and tea.

    How the goal is worked out

    It starts from Fidelity’s savings guideline: save at least 1× your yearly income by age 30, 3× by 40, 6× by 50, 8× by 60, and 10× by 67.

    The app aims a little lower, at 80% of those amounts: 8× your income by 67. That’s the amount Fidelity gives for someone who plans to live on a bit less in retirement, or to retire at 70 instead of 67.

    Each age range uses the middle of the range, and each income range uses its middle amount. So the goal is only a rough guide, not financial advice.

    Sizes and weights are estimates.Keeping it light

    Each figure is typical for compact gear, rounded. Real packing leaves gaps, so leave a few liters free.

    The bag size is the smallest of 20, 25, or 30 L that the list fills no more than three quarters of, leaving the rest for gaps and personal items. It depends on the bag’s type and your Local Hazards.

    A common hiking guideline is to keep a loaded pack under about 20% of your body weight. Water is the heaviest thing in the bag, at about 2.2 lb per liter.

    A 30 L pack is a large daypack: small enough to carry all day, to keep under a desk or in a car, and to grab on the way out the door.

    Packing list

    Tick things off as they go in the bag. Your Local Hazards come first. The bag is tracked on its own, so nothing here changes your inventory on the Preps pages.

    Packing tips
    • Put heavy things, like water and food, in the middle of the bag and close to your back.
    • Keep what you’ll reach for often, like the headlamp, first aid kit, map, and rain jacket, near the top or in outside pockets.
    • Keep the bag where you spend your days: at work or in the car for getting home, and by the door when you’re home. Moving it between them is part of the plan.
    • Check it every 6 months. Replace food, water, medications, and batteries before they expire, and swap clothing for the season.
    • Walk part of your route home with the bag on once, to check that it’s comfortable and how long it takes.

    Choosing a bag

    Features, budget, and the two jobs

    Any sturdy backpack of 30 liters or less can do the job. What matters most is that it fits you and carries comfortably for hours.

    Worth having

    • Padded shoulder straps and a padded back panel.
    • A hip belt and chest strap, which move weight off your shoulders.
    • Durable fabric and sturdy zippers, the parts most likely to fail.
    • Water resistance, from the fabric or a rain cover.
    • A fit that suits your torso length. Try it on loaded if you can.

    Nice to have

    • Outside pockets for things you reach for often.
    • Compression straps to keep a part-full bag from shifting.
    • A hydration sleeve, attachment loops, or webbing.
    • Lighter or tougher specialty materials, and a long warranty.
    Don’t chase features past your budget.

    Each added feature tends to raise the price, and often the weight, while adding less and less to what the bag can do. A basic daypack that fits well and holds up is enough. If a feature would push a bag outside your budget, skip it and put the money toward what goes inside.

    One bag, two jobs

    Get home and get out
    Get home

    You’re at work, school, or on the road when something happens, and need to make it back, maybe on foot, over a day or two.

    Get out

    You need to leave home quickly and get by on your own until you reach shelter or more supplies. The Red Cross suggests 3 days of supplies for an evacuation kit, but a 30 L bag can’t carry 3 days of water, so treat 72 hours as an absolute maximum, not what to expect.

    Most of the list does both jobs, marked Both. The rest is tagged with the job it’s mainly for. One bag means one set of gear to buy, pack, and keep up to date.

    What Prep Tracker does

    • Keeps track of your supplies. Water, Food, Medical, and the other categories each have their own page in the ☰ menu. Add what you have, with how much, where it’s stored, and when it expires. On the Food page, you can scan a barcode to fill in the details.
    • Watches expiration dates for you. Leave the date blank and the app estimates one from the type of item. The Overview shows anything expired or expiring within 60 days, so you can use it and replace it in time.
    • Suggests what to get next. Choose how long you’re preparing for: a week, a month, a year, or beyond. Each page then lists supplies that fit that goal, and you can add them to your inventory as you get them. You can also choose Local Hazards, like winter storms or wildfire, for a few more suggestions.
    • Helps you build savings. The Savings page tracks your emergency cash, with a goal sized to how long you’re preparing for, and your investments, with a rough goal based on your age and income ranges.
    • Celebrates your progress. Once a year, on the anniversary of when you started, the Overview offers a look back at how your supplies and savings have grown. You can open it any time from the Settings page.
    • Lists plans and skills. The Plans and Skills page covers things to set up that you can’t buy, like a family communication plan. The Grab list keeps an ordered list of what to take if you ever need to leave in a hurry, with boxes to tick off. Quick reference has short how-tos, like purifying water or keeping food safe without power, that work with no connection. During an outage, low-battery mode (in the ☰ menu) shows just the essentials on a black screen that uses less power.

    Important: export your data regularly

    Prep Tracker stores everything only in this browser, on this device. Nothing is saved online.

    If your browser data is cleared, your browser is reset or reinstalled, or you move to a new phone or computer, everything you’ve entered will be gone unless you have a backup.

    To keep it safe, open Backup in Settings and tap Export backup every so often, for example once a month or after adding a batch of items. Keep the file somewhere you won’t lose it, such as your email, cloud storage, or another computer. If you ever need it, tap Import backup in Settings to bring everything back.

    Why savings matter most

    Stored supplies get you through the first days of a disruption. The longer it lasts, the more your savings matter. Most long disruptions are financial ones: a job loss, an illness, rising prices, or a slow local economy. Money is what carries a household through them.

    • It fits any situation. Supplies cover the needs you planned for. Money also covers the ones you didn’t: a hotel during an evacuation, a car repair, a replacement for something that broke.
    • It doesn’t expire. Savings don’t need rotating, storage space, or a cool, dry shelf.
    • It keeps working after the stores reopen. Most disruptions end with shelves restocked but bills still due. Savings cover the weeks or months it takes to get back on your feet.
    • It gives you time. With a cushion, you can make decisions calmly instead of in a hurry.

    Building it steadily

    • Small, regular amounts add up. A steady weekly habit matters more than a big start.
    • Keep some cash at home in small bills for when card readers are down.
    • Emergency cash is the money you can reach right away. Investments can rise and fall in value and can take a few days to sell, so they work best as the longer-term layer behind your cash.
    • Grow savings and supplies together, and avoid going into debt for supplies. Debt is its own emergency.
